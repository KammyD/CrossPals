## The situation

A US-based Amazon seller in Kitchen & Dining had run one order of 500 units through a factory they had found themselves. Roughly 30% arrived defective — cracked handles, loose fittings, packaging that had already split in transit. Amazon received the shipment, but the returns started inside the first two weeks and the listing rating dropped with them.

Their plan for the next order was simple: same factory, same product, same price, reorder. The working assumption was that the defects had been a one-off.

## The problem with "one-off"

A 30% defect rate is rarely random. Either the mould was worn, the material was substituted, or the factory had changed something in the process to hit the price. All three are invisible in a sample the factory sends you — and all three are cheap to find with a physical inspection at the factory, before anything is packed.

## What we did

We booked a pre-shipment inspection at the factory once the batch was finished and packed, using a statistically valid sample against a 2.5 AQL for major defects.

The batch failed. Counting by class, we found:

- **Major** — cracked handles and loose fittings, above the AQL limit
- **Minor** — surface scuffs and uneven finish, inside tolerance
- **Packaging** — inner cartons under-spec, would not survive a container

We photographed every finding, gave the supplier a written defect list, and held the order. No packing, no balance payment.

The supplier reworked the batch with a corrected mould and re-packed the inner cartons. We re-inspected the rework, then ran the FBA prep: FNSKU labels applied flat and scanned unit by unit, poly-bags checked for the suffocation warning, cartons weighed and measured against the shipment plan, packing list reconciled box by box.

## The result

15 defective units were caught and corrected before the order shipped. From supplier rework to FBA-ready the order took 18 days. Nothing was refused at Amazon receiving, and the seller has reordered twice since.

## The part that repeats

The failure was not the factory — it was placing a repeat order without a check. The second order cost $150 in inspection and saved a 500-unit rework at sea. That is the whole trade.
