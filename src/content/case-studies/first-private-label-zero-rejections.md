## The situation

A first-time Amazon seller in Spain wanted to launch a private-label pet product. They had never imported from China, had no factory contacts, and — reasonably — were wary of sending money to a company they had only met through a chat window.

Their list of unknowns was the standard one: which factory is real, whether the sample matches the mass production run, and what an Amazon warehouse actually expects to receive.

## What we did

**Factory verification.** We shortlisted three factories making the product and vetted each one: business licence, registered address, export history, and a live video walkthrough of the production line. One dropped out — the address on the licence did not match the workshop. The seller chose from the remaining two on price and tooling, not on guesswork.

**Sample confirmation.** The chosen factory sent a pre-production sample. We checked it against the agreed spec — material, dimensions, stitching, finish — and photographed it against a ruler and a colour card, so the approved sample became a documented benchmark rather than a memory.

**Mass production inspection.** Once the run was finished we inspected the batch at the factory, same five standards: appearance, dimensions, function, packaging, FBA label check.

**FBA prep.** Then the part first-time sellers usually under-estimate:

- FNSKU label applied flat to each unit, existing barcodes covered, every label scanned
- Poly-bags sealed with the suffocation warning in place
- Cartons weighed and measured against Amazon's per-carton limits
- Box labels and packing list matched to the shipment plan, box by box

## The result

800 units, one order, zero FBA rejections. From purchase order to delivery at the seller's door: 34 days.

The seller's own summary was that the inspection report was the first document that made the whole order feel real — they could see the actual units that were about to be theirs.

## Why this case is worth reading

Nothing here was clever. Factory verification, an approved sample, one inspection, correct prep. That is the entire job — and it is exactly the sequence a first order skips when it goes wrong.
