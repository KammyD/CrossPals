A pre-shipment inspection report is only useful if you can read it. A vague "looks good" tells you nothing; a structured report tells you exactly how much risk you are about to accept. Here is what to look for, in order.

## Sample size and AQL

Nobody inspects 100% of a large batch. Inspectors draw a statistically valid sample — for example, 80 units out of 1,000 — and grade it against an AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit), commonly 2.5 for major defects. **Check:** the report states the sample size, the AQL level, and the standard used (e.g. ISO 2859 / GB 2828).

## Defect classes

Good reports split defects into **critical** (unsafe, illegal, unusable), **major** (the customer would return it), and **minor** (cosmetic, noticeable but tolerable). A batch that "passes" can still carry minor defects — the question is whether the counts sit inside the AQL limits. **Check:** defects are counted by class, not described in adjectives.

## Photo evidence

Every finding should be photographed: the good, the bad, and the packaging. A report with 30–40 photos lets you see the batch yourself. A report with three photos is a summary, not evidence. **Check:** photos cover the defect, the product in hand, and the packed carton.

## The five standards

A complete inspection covers appearance, dimensions, function, packaging, and — for FBA sellers — the label check. If a report skips function testing or label verification, it is not a complete inspection. **Check:** all five are addressed.

## What to do when it fails

A failed inspection is not a disaster — it is the whole point. You now hold the supplier to a rework, a replacement, or a partial refund, **before** final payment. Never release the balance until the failure is resolved and re-verified.
